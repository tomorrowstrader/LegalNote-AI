The core principle
Decision makers at boutique firms don't buy features. They buy protection from consequences they're already afraid of. The entire Compliance Thread, the Matter Inception Record, Log a Call — none of that should ever be mentioned by name in a networking conversation or early demo. What should be mentioned is the moment they dread.

Networking event — the one question that does all the work
You don't need a pitch. You need a question.

"When the SRA reviews your AML files, how much of the reasoning behind your solicitors' decisions is actually documented — not just the forms, but why each call was made?"

That's it. Then you stop talking.

Every managing partner in the room knows the honest answer. They'll either admit the gap directly or deflect with something that tells you they know it's there. Either way, you've made them feel the problem before you've mentioned a product.

Then one follow-up:

"That's what LegalNote solves. Every client meeting is recorded and formatted into a defensible attendance note — but it also builds a continuous documented record of AML decision-making, from the first conversation right through to closing the file. Not more admin. It happens as your solicitors already work."

That's the entire networking conversation. If they want more, they'll ask. The worst thing you can do is answer questions they haven't asked yet.

Live demo — the cardinal rule
Never demo features. Demo a scenario. Specifically, demo the moment they fear most.

Open with: "Let me show you what happens the morning the SRA calls and asks to review your client file for the Patterson matter."

Then walk through LegalNote showing exactly what gets handed to the auditor:

Open the case — the Compliance Thread is visible immediately. One panel, complete picture.
Click the Matter Inception Record from the first client meeting — source of funds discussed, risk assessment reasoned, EDD decision with the solicitor's own words explaining why. Dated, signed, tamper-proof.
Jump to a mid-matter meeting note — "Our system detected something in this meeting that might affect the AML position and surfaced a prompt. The fee earner added a monitoring note here. That note is now part of the file."
Show the MLRO referral — fee earner flagged a concern, MLRO reviewed, decision documented with reasoning. Timestamp, signature.
Pull back to the timeline — "This is what you hand to the SRA. Everything documented as it happened. Nothing reconstructed. Nothing missing."
Close with: "That's spread across six minutes of additional work over months of client relationship — captured automatically as part of how your firm already operates. Compare that to trying to reconstruct the same picture two years later from memory and sparse matter notes."

Total demo time: four to five minutes. Show one scenario and stop. Leaving them wanting more is the point.

The phrase that anchors everything
Defensible by design.

Not defensible because your solicitors worked harder. Not defensible because your forms are better. Defensible because the documentation built itself as part of normal working practice. That phrase works in a conversation, on a slide, in an email follow-up, anywhere.

What to avoid in both contexts
Never lead with AI — it triggers scepticism in solicitors and signals that a computer is making decisions about their compliance
Never describe features by their technical names — trigger event detection, MLRO referral workflow — these sound like software, not solutions
Never say the word "compliance" as a selling word — it's overused in this sector and associates immediately with cost and burden, not protection
Never show more than one scenario in a demo — attention fractures the moment they feel they're being given a tour rather than shown something specific
Never let the demo become a Q&A about features mid-flow — hold questions until the scenario lands
The audience needs to feel one thing by the end: "If the SRA came tomorrow, I would not have this." That feeling is what books the follow-up conversation.
