Someone reported my diamond earring at Clifford Chance.

Not to my face. To my manager.

My first day at one of the most prestigious law firms in the world. I came in, did my work, built rapport with the team. Felt good about it.

The next morning, my manager pulled me aside. Asked how I found my first day. I said it went well.

Then he told me. Someone had raised an issue with me wearing a diamond earring.

Nobody said it to me directly. Nobody explained which policy it broke. It was just decided, behind my back, that something about how I presented myself wasn’t acceptable.

This is why corporate never felt like home.

Not because the work was bad. The work was excellent. The teams were often brilliant. But there was always an undercurrent. A lack of sincerity that I could never fully ignore.

It took me back to my time in music school. At 13, despite achieving the highest marks—10 distinctions and high honors—I was the first and only person to be removed from the scholarship program. That was a pivotal age when kids start looking more like young adults, possibly carrying the mannerisms from their environments, in my case, the vibrant estates of London.

At the time, maybe I didn’t fully understand the importance of adapting to different environments. Retrospectively, my removal by judgment felt more like a reaction to change rather than performance, a scrutiny others seemed exempt from. But, my journey through music, to Coutts, and eventually other prestigious settings taught me growth and adaptation.

The results were in question back then, but I learned. I learned to adapt and thrive in varied environments, confident that my presentation and work speak for themselves.

People in the same role for fifteen years with no ambition of their own, but quietly ensuring you didn’t get too far either. Saying one thing in meetings, another in corridors.

I spent years navigating that. Learning from it. Building relationships despite it.

And eventually, I understood: I could either spend my career fighting that culture, or I could build my own.

I chose to build.
