Someone reported my diamond earring at Clifford Chance.

Not to my face. To my manager.

My first day at one of the most prestigious law firms in the world. I came in, did my work, built rapport with the team. Felt good about it.

The next morning, my manager pulled me aside. Asked how I found my first day. I said it went well.

Then she told me. Someone had raised an issue with me wearing a diamond earring.

Nobody said it to me directly. Nobody explained which policy it broke. It was just decided, behind my back, that something about how I presented myself wasn’t acceptable.

This is why corporate never felt like home.

Not because the work was bad. The work was excellent. The teams were often brilliant. But there was always an undercurrent. A quiet dishonesty I could never fully ignore.

It took me back to music school. At 13, I achieved the highest marks — 10 distinctions and high honours over 5 years. I was also the first and only person removed from the scholarship program.

Same pattern. The results were never in question. It was who I was that drew scrutiny others seemed exempt from.

That experience stayed with me. Not as resentment. As awareness.

I realised early that I couldn’t control how people judged me. But I could make myself impossible to question.

So I did. I became a sharper version of myself. Not diluted. Not performing. Just more intentional. The kind of person who walked into any room and left no gap for scrutiny.

Not because I owed anyone that. Because I owed it to myself.

People in the same role for fifteen years with no ambition of their own, but quietly ensuring you didn’t get too far either. Saying one thing in meetings, another in corridors.

I spent years navigating that. Learning from it. Thriving in it. Building relationships despite it.

And eventually, I understood: I could either spend my career fighting that culture, or I could build my own.

I chose to build. A company where documentation speaks louder than perception. Where the work is the proof.
