#### Post 9 — Monday, Week 3
**Theme:** Your attendance notes wouldn't survive an audit
**Voice:** The Compliance Professional
**Framework:** PAS

```
Most attendance notes are written from memory.

That's not documentation. That's recollection.

There's a fundamental difference between a record created during or immediately after an event, and one reconstructed hours later from what someone remembers.

In regulated environments like banking, we call the first one contemporaneous documentation. It's treated as evidence.

The second one? In an audit, it's treated as opinion.

Here's what I observed working inside highly regulated organisations:

1. Timestamps matter. A note created 4 hours after a meeting carries less weight than one created during.
2. Accuracy degrades with time. Studies show memory distortion begins within 20 minutes.
3. Audit trails exist or they don't. "I wrote it that day" isn't provable without a system that logs it.

Most boutique law firms are advising clients on defensibility and compliance.

But their own documentation workflow — the attendance note — would fail the standard they're advising on.

That's the gap LegalNote closes. Contemporaneous. Timestamped. Tamper-evident.

Not because it's fancy. Because that's what evidence requires.

PS — If your attendance notes were audited tomorrow, would they hold up?
```

#### Post 19 — Thursday, Week 5
**Theme:** Recording meetings isn't the problem — consent is
**Voice:** The Compliance Professional
**Framework:** PAS

```
Recording meetings isn't new.

Doing it compliantly in legal practice is.

Every solicitor I speak to has the same initial reaction: "We can't record client meetings."

But when I ask why, the answer is rarely about law. It's about culture. Tradition. The way it's always been done.

The reality is that recording a meeting with informed consent is entirely lawful. The question isn't whether you can. It's whether you're doing it properly.

Properly means:

1. Explicit, informed consent captured before recording begins
2. Clear explanation of what the recording will be used for
3. Client's right to withdraw consent at any point
4. Data processed and stored in compliance with UK GDPR
5. Retention policies that minimise data holding
6. Audit trails that prove the above happened

That's what most firms don't have. Not the recording itself. The infrastructure around it.

LegalNote makes consent the starting point, not an afterthought. The verification is baked into the workflow. The audit trail is automatic. The data stays in the EU.

Technology has made compliant recording possible in a way it wasn't five years ago. The culture just hasn't caught up yet.

PS — What would change about your practice if you could compliantly record every client meeting?
```

**Theme:** Why UK attendance notes are globally unique
**Voice:** The Compliance Professional
**Framework:** SLAY

```
The UK is the only jurisdiction I know of where attendance notes are essentially mandatory.

That makes this problem uniquely British. And uniquely solvable.

After every client meeting, UK solicitors are expected to produce a written record of what was discussed, what advice was given, and what was agreed. The SRA expects it. PI insurers rely on it. The courts reference it.

In other jurisdictions, the standards are different. The requirements are looser. The documentation culture varies.

But in the UK, the attendance note is the backbone of professional accountability for solicitors.

And yet — the process for creating that note hasn't fundamentally changed in decades.

Meeting happens. Solicitor writes it up afterwards. Client receives it days later. No audio reference. No contemporaneous capture. No audit trail.

For something so important to professional practice, the tooling is remarkably basic.

That's not a criticism. It's an opportunity.

LegalNote is built specifically for this requirement. Not adapted from a general meeting tool. Not a US product modified for UK compliance.

Built for the attendance note. Built for UK solicitors. Built for the standard they're already held to.

PS — Do you think the process of creating attendance notes has kept up with the rest of legal practice?
```
